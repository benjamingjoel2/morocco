Every year, someone asks us whether they should avoid Morocco during Ramadan. Our answer is almost always no. Travelling during the holy month means adjusting your expectations and your daily rhythm, but it also gives you a glimpse of Moroccan life that most visitors never see. Here is what to expect and how to travel well.

## What Ramadan is

Ramadan is the ninth month of the Islamic calendar, a month of fasting, prayer, reflection and community. Between dawn and sunset, observant Muslims abstain from food, drink (including water), smoking and sexual relations. The fast is broken at sunset with a meal called iftar, and many people eat again before dawn, at a meal called suhoor.

Morocco is a predominantly Muslim country, and Ramadan shapes daily life across the whole kingdom, in cities and villages alike.

### When is it?

Ramadan follows the lunar Islamic calendar, which is shorter than the solar year. As a result, it moves about 11 days earlier each year, gradually travelling through the seasons. The start and end are confirmed by the sighting of the new moon, and in Morocco they are announced officially, so the dates can differ by a day from neighbouring countries.

We are not going to give dates here, because they change every year. If you're planning a trip, check the expected dates for your year, and allow a day either side.

The month ends with **Eid al-Fitr**, a festive holiday when families gather, dress up and visit one another. Expect some businesses and services to close for a day or two around Eid, and transport to be busy as people travel home.

## How daily life changes

### Daytime

During the day, the pace slows. People are fasting, often tired, and may be working shorter hours. In practice, that means:

- Many shops, offices and some sights open later and close earlier than usual. Check hours locally rather than relying on what you've read online.
- Many cafés and local restaurants close during daylight hours, particularly in smaller towns.
- Restaurants aimed at tourists, hotel restaurants and riads generally continue to serve food to non-fasting guests, sometimes more discreetly.
- Traffic can be chaotic in the hour before sunset, as everyone rushes home for iftar. It's a good time to be off the roads.

### Sunset

The moment the fast breaks is something to experience. In the last half-hour, the streets empty. Then, at sunset, the call to prayer sounds and the city exhales. Families sit down together and an extraordinary quiet descends for a while.

### Evening

After iftar, the cities come back to life. Streets fill, cafés open, families stroll and the medinas take on a festive, late-night atmosphere that can last well past midnight. If you enjoy evenings out, this is a lovely time to be in Marrakech or Fès.

You may also hear the **nafar** in the small hours: a man who walks through the neighbourhood with a long trumpet or a drum to wake people for suhoor. It's a tradition that has largely disappeared elsewhere, and it's quite something to hear.

## Food during Ramadan

Ramadan is, perhaps surprisingly, one of the best times to eat in Morocco. Iftar has its own traditional dishes, and pastry shops and markets fill with seasonal specialities. A typical Moroccan iftar might include:

- **Dates** and a glass of milk or juice, often the first thing eaten
- **Harira**, a hearty tomato, lentil and chickpea soup, the centrepiece of the table
- **Chebakia**, flower-shaped pastries fried and soaked in honey, scented with sesame
- **Sellou**, a rich mixture of toasted flour, almonds and sesame
- **Msemen** and **baghrir**, flaky square flatbreads and spongy semolina pancakes
- Hard-boiled eggs, olives and plenty of mint tea

If you're staying in a riad, ask whether your hosts serve iftar. Some invite guests to share it, and it's one of the warmest experiences you can have in Morocco. For more on the dishes, see [what to do and eat](/things-to-do/).

## Etiquette for visitors

You're not expected to fast, and nobody will mind that you're eating. But a little consideration goes a long way.

- **Eat and drink discreetly in public during the day.** Moroccan law prohibits Muslims from publicly breaking the fast; it isn't applied to foreign visitors, but eating, drinking or smoking openly in the street is considered impolite. Carry water, but drink it out of sight where you can.
- **Dress modestly.** This matters more than ever during Ramadan. Cover shoulders and knees, whatever your gender.
- **Be patient.** People are fasting and may be tired, especially in the late afternoon. A little extra patience is appreciated.
- **Avoid public displays of affection,** which are frowned upon in Morocco at the best of times.
- **Expect less alcohol.** Many shops and supermarkets stop selling alcohol during Ramadan, and some places that normally serve it don't. Some hotels continue to serve non-Muslim guests.
- **Mosques:** As at any time of year, most mosques in Morocco are closed to non-Muslims.

A simple "Ramadan mubarak" or "Ramadan karim" as a greeting is always appreciated.

## Practicalities

### Transport and tours

Trains and buses continue to run, though timetables can change, and there may be a lull around sunset. Drivers and guides will generally work as normal, but you might plan shorter days or longer lunch breaks, allowing them time to break their fast. Ask how they'd like to arrange things. Many will have a preference.

### Sights and museums

Most major sights stay open, often with shortened hours. Check opening times locally before you go.

### The countryside

Rural areas and smaller towns tend to be quieter during the day, with fewer places to eat. If you're trekking in the [High Atlas](/destinations/atlas/) or heading towards the [Sahara](/destinations/sahara/), make sure your guide or accommodation will be providing meals.

## Laylat al-Qadr

The Night of Power commemorates the night the first verses of the Quran were revealed to the Prophet Muhammad. In Morocco, it is widely observed on the 27th night of Ramadan, with mosques full and families gathering. Children are sometimes dressed in traditional clothing to mark the occasion. It's a beautiful night to be in a city, as long as you keep a respectful distance.

## Should you go?

If you want everything open all day and a busy bar scene, Ramadan isn't the best month for you. But if you're happy to slow down, plan around the rhythm of the day and spend your evenings out, it can be a deeply rewarding time to visit, and the welcome is often warmer than ever.
