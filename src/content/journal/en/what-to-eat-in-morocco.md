Moroccan food is one of the great pleasures of travelling here, but it's easy to eat the same chicken tagine every night and go home thinking you've tried it all. The best of it is often found at home tables, in market stalls and in the dishes that only appear on certain days or in certain seasons. Here is what to look for.

## The essentials

### Tagine

The word refers both to the dish and to the conical clay pot it's cooked in. The pot traps steam, so the ingredients cook slowly in their own juices. There are endless combinations, but a few classics are worth seeking out:

- **Chicken with preserved lemon and olives**, sharp, salty and fragrant
- **Lamb or beef with prunes and almonds**, sweet and often finished with sesame seeds
- **Kefta tagine**, spiced meatballs in tomato sauce, often with an egg cracked on top
- **Vegetable tagine**, a good everyday option for vegetarians

A good tagine takes time, so the best versions are often in homes, riads and modest local places where it's been on the fire for hours.

### Couscous

Couscous is traditionally a Friday dish, eaten with family after midday prayers. The semolina is steamed several times over a pot of stew, then piled high with slow-cooked meat and vegetables, sometimes with a sweet topping of caramelised onions and raisins known as tfaya. In 2020, the knowledge and practices around couscous were added to UNESCO's intangible cultural heritage list, in a joint nomination by Morocco, Algeria, Mauritania and Tunisia.

If you're in Morocco on a Friday, ask your riad or a local restaurant whether they're serving couscous. Many places only make it that day.

### Harira

This tomato, lentil and chickpea soup, thickened and fragrant with herbs and spices, is everyday comfort food and the traditional dish for breaking the fast during Ramadan. It's often served with dates and a sweet pastry. A bowl from a simple café is one of the cheapest and most satisfying meals you'll have.

### Pastilla

A dish for special occasions: layers of very thin pastry (warqa) filled with spiced meat, almonds and egg, baked and dusted with icing sugar and cinnamon. The classic version is made with pigeon, though chicken is more common now. The mixture of sweet and savoury surprises many first-timers. There's also a seafood pastilla, without the sugar, which is popular on the coast.

### Tanjia

The signature dish of Marrakech. Beef or lamb is packed into a tall clay urn with preserved lemon, garlic, spices and a little smen (aged butter), then traditionally left to cook overnight in the embers of the furnace that heats a neighbourhood hammam. It comes out meltingly soft. It's traditionally a men's dish, made for gatherings and Friday outings, but you'll find it served in plenty of simple places around the medina. ### Mechoui

Whole lamb, slow-roasted until the meat falls apart, eaten with cumin and salt. In Marrakech, there's a stretch near Jemaa el-Fnaa where it's sold by weight at lunchtime.

## Breakfast and bread

A Moroccan breakfast is one of the joys of staying in a riad. Expect some combination of:

- **Msemen**, square, flaky, pan-fried flatbreads
- **Baghrir**, the "thousand-hole" semolina pancakes that soak up honey and butter
- **Harcha**, a slightly crumbly semolina griddle bread
- **Khobz**, the round everyday bread, which also doubles as your cutlery at most meals
- **Amlou**, a spread of argan oil, roasted almonds and honey from the south-west, addictive on warm bread
- Olive oil, olives, fresh cheese, eggs and fresh orange juice

## Salads and starters

Moroccan "salads" are usually cooked vegetable dishes served in small bowls at the start of a meal. Look out for:

- **Zaalouk**, smoky aubergine with tomato, garlic and cumin
- **Taktouka**, peppers and tomatoes cooked down together
- **Carrot salad** with cumin, orange blossom water or cinnamon
- **Bissara**, a thick fava bean soup or dip drizzled with olive oil and cumin, a winter breakfast staple in the north

## Street food

Street food is where you'll eat with locals. Some favourites:

- **Snail soup**, served from carts in the evening, in a broth with a long list of spices said to aid digestion
- **Sfenj**, Moroccan doughnuts, fried to order in the morning
- **Grilled brochettes** and kefta, with bread, onion and spices
- **Msemen** stuffed with onion or minced meat, from morning stalls
- **Fried fish**, in coastal towns like Essaouira, where you can choose from the catch at the port and have it grilled nearby

The usual rule applies: choose stalls that are busy, where food is cooked fresh in front of you.

## Sweets and pastries

Moroccan pastries are often made with almonds, honey and orange blossom water. Try **cornes de gazelle** (kaab el ghazal), crescent-shaped pastries filled with almond paste; **chebakia**, honey-soaked sesame flowers, especially at Ramadan; and **briouats**, small triangles of pastry with sweet or savoury fillings.

## Mint tea

Mint tea is the backbone of Moroccan hospitality. It's made with Chinese gunpowder green tea, a big handful of fresh spearmint and, traditionally, plenty of sugar. It's poured from a height to create a little foam on top. You'll be offered it in homes, shops and riads. Accept it when you can, and if you prefer it less sweet, ask for "shwiya sukkar" (a little sugar) before it's made.

## How to eat

- **Use your right hand.** At home and at communal dishes, people traditionally eat with the right hand, using bread to scoop food from the shared plate. Stick to the section of the dish in front of you.
- **Wash your hands.** In homes and many traditional restaurants, a basin and kettle are brought to the table for washing hands before the meal.
- **Pace yourself.** At a family meal or a festive dinner, there may be several courses. Your hosts will keep offering more; it's fine to politely decline.
- **Bread is precious.** Don't waste it, and don't put it on the floor.

## Eating safely

Most travellers eat very well in Morocco without trouble. A few sensible habits help:

- Drink bottled or filtered water and be cautious with ice in simple places.
- Choose food that's freshly cooked and served hot.
- Peel fruit, or wash it in safe water.
- Ease in: rich, oily food in the heat can upset a stomach in the first few days.

## Vegetarians and other diets

Vegetarians do well in Morocco, thanks to the vegetable tagines, couscous with seven vegetables, salads, soups and breads. Do check with your host, though, as stews are sometimes made with meat stock. Vegans and those with allergies should plan ahead and ask your accommodation, as butter, smen and nuts are common.
