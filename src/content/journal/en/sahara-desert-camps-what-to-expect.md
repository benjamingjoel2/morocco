A night in the dunes is the moment many travellers picture when they first think of Morocco, and it can be every bit as good as you hope. The silence, the stars, the light sliding across the sand at sunrise. But it helps to know what you're signing up for, because the reality involves long drives, a wide range of camps and nights colder than most people expect. Here is the honest version.

## Where are the dunes?

Morocco's best-known sand seas are in the south-east, on the edge of the Sahara. There are two main choices.

### Erg Chebbi, near Merzouga

Erg Chebbi is the larger and more accessible of the two. The dunes rise dramatically behind the village of Merzouga and change colour through the day, from pale gold to deep orange. Because a paved road reaches Merzouga, it's easier to get to and has the widest range of camps, from simple to very comfortable. The trade-off is that it's busier, and on some evenings you'll see other camps' lights in the distance.

### Erg Chigaga, beyond M'Hamid

Erg Chigaga lies further south-west, beyond the small town of M'Hamid el Ghizlane at the end of the road through the Draa Valley. The final stretch is off-road, usually by 4x4, which keeps numbers down. It feels more remote and quieter, with fewer camps scattered over a wide area. It suits travellers who prioritise solitude and don't mind a bumpier, more adventurous approach.

## The journey there

This is the part that surprises people. From Marrakech, the drive to Merzouga is long: most itineraries split it over two days, with an overnight stop along the way. The route crosses the High Atlas via the Tizi n'Tichka pass, then drops through the Ouarzazate region with its kasbahs, palm groves and gorges.

The good news is that the road is a journey in its own right. Common stops include:

- **Aït Benhaddou**, the fortified earthen village (ksar) listed by UNESCO, which you may recognise from films.
- **The Dades Valley and Todra Gorge**, with their dramatic rock walls and green river oases.
- **The Draa Valley**, a long ribbon of date palms on the way to M'Hamid and Erg Chigaga.

Coming from Fès, you cross the Middle Atlas instead, through cedar forests and down into the Ziz Valley. It's also a long day, but just as beautiful. Look at our [routes](/routes/) to see how a desert trip slots into a wider itinerary.

We'd gently discourage the "Sahara in two days from Marrakech" trips if you can avoid them. They are possible, but you'll spend most of your time in a vehicle and arrive too tired to enjoy it. Three days is a more comfortable minimum, and more is better.

## Arriving at camp

Most camps in Erg Chebbi are reached by camel or by 4x4, and many travellers combine the two: camels in, vehicle out, or the reverse. The camel ride is usually an hour or two, timed so you arrive around sunset.

### About the camels

Moroccan camels are dromedaries, with one hump. Riding one is a rocking, slightly undignified experience, particularly as they stand up and sit down. A few tips:

- Lean back as the camel rises and lean back again as it kneels.
- Wear long trousers; the saddles can chafe.
- If you have back problems or simply don't fancy it, ask for a 4x4 transfer or walk alongside. Nobody will mind.

It's worth asking your organiser how the animals are cared for. Healthy, well-fed camels with proper saddles and reasonable workloads are a good sign of a responsible operation.

## What the camps are like

"Desert camp" covers a wide range, and the difference in comfort and price is considerable.

### Standard camps

These typically have simple tents made of woven fabric or canvas, with beds and blankets, and shared toilet and washing facilities. They're comfortable enough for a night, and the experience of the dunes is the same.

### Comfortable and "luxury" camps

At the upper end, expect larger tents with proper beds, rugs, private bathrooms with hot showers and sometimes electricity from solar panels. They cost noticeably more, and they're worth it if you're travelling with children, staying more than one night, or visiting in the colder months.

Whichever you choose, ask what's included: dinner and breakfast are usually part of the package, but drinks, sandboarding and extra activities may not be.

## An evening in the dunes

After sunset, dinner is usually served in a communal tent or under the sky: often a harira or vegetable soup, then a tagine, then fruit. Afterwards many camps light a fire, and the staff may play music on drums and sing. Around Merzouga, Gnawa music has deep roots, particularly in the nearby village of Khamlia.

Then step away from the fire and look up. On a clear, moonless night, the stars are extraordinary. If stargazing is a priority, check the moon phase before you book: a full moon is beautiful in its own way, but it washes out the fainter stars.

## Sunrise

Set an alarm. Climbing a dune before dawn is hard work (two steps forward, one step back), but watching the light come up over the sand is the reason many people make the trip. Go slowly, choose a ridge and take your time.

## When to go

- **Spring and autumn** are the most comfortable seasons, with warm days and cool nights.
- **Summer** is extremely hot. Many travellers avoid the desert entirely in the hottest months, and those who go should plan activities for early morning and evening.
- **Winter** brings pleasant days but cold nights, which can approach freezing. Good camps provide heavy blankets; extra layers are still wise.
- **Wind** can whip up sand at any time of year, particularly in spring. It usually passes, but it can obscure a sunset.

## What to pack

- Warm layers, a hat and a scarf for the evening, even outside winter
- A headtorch
- Sunglasses and high-factor sun cream
- A scarf or buff to cover your face if the wind picks up
- A small overnight bag; your main luggage can often stay in the vehicle
- Wet wipes and any medication you need, as facilities are limited

Phone signal varies from camp to camp, so tell people at home you may be out of contact for a night.

## Is it worth it?

Yes, if you build in enough time. Rushed, it can feel like a long drive to a crowded photo stop. Given three or four days, with stops along the way and perhaps two nights in the dunes, it can be the highlight of your trip.
