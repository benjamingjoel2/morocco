An hour or two south of Marrakech, the plain gives way to the High Atlas, and everything changes. The air cools, the noise drops away and the road climbs through walnut trees and terraced fields to Imlil, a village at the foot of North Africa's highest mountain. It's the most popular base for walking in Morocco, and for good reason. Here is how to make the most of it.

## Getting there

Imlil is usually reached by car from Marrakech, a journey of roughly one and a half to two hours depending on traffic and stops. The road passes through the town of Asni before winding up the valley. Most travellers arrange a private transfer through their accommodation or guide; shared taxis also run, with a change in Asni.

The village sits at roughly 1,700–1,800 metres, so even in summer it is noticeably cooler than Marrakech. Bring a warm layer, however hot it was when you left.

## A word about the earthquake

The Al Haouz earthquake of September 2023 struck this region hard, and many villages in and around the Imlil valley were badly damaged. Rebuilding has been under way since then, and the area has welcomed visitors again, but the scars are still visible in places. Walking here with local guides and staying in locally run houses is one of the most direct ways to support the recovery. Ask your guide about it if you'd like to; many are glad to talk about how their villages are coping.

## Choosing your walk

### Half-day and day walks

You don't need to be a serious hiker to enjoy the Imlil valley. Easy walks lead through villages, orchards and terraces along the irrigation channels that keep the fields green. Popular options include:

- **Aroumd (Armed)**, a village on a spur above Imlil, with wide views up towards the higher peaks.
- **The Tizi n'Tamatert pass**, a steady climb with views back over the valley, and a route on towards the Ourika Valley.
- **Valley circuits** through neighbouring villages, often ending with mint tea and lunch in a family home.

A guide adds a great deal on these walks: they know the paths, the families and the stories, and they'll make sure you don't wander onto private land or into someone's field.

### Multi-day village treks

The real magic of the Atlas is in walking from village to village over several days, sleeping in gîtes (simple mountain guesthouses) or homestays. Mules carry the luggage, a cook often comes along, and the rhythm of the day becomes walking, eating and resting.

Routes from Imlil can link villages such as Tacheddirt and Imlil with the Ourika and Azzaden valleys, crossing passes along the way. Two to five days is common. These treks suit most reasonably fit walkers, and the pace can be adjusted to suit you.

### Climbing Toubkal

At 4,167 metres, Jebel Toubkal is the highest peak in North Africa, and it lies at the heart of Toubkal National Park. It's not a technical climb in summer, but it is a demanding one, and altitude is a real factor.

The standard route usually takes two days:

1. **Day one:** walk from Imlil past the shrine of Sidi Chamharouch to the mountain refuges at around 3,200 metres. Expect several hours of steady uphill walking.
2. **Day two:** an early start for the summit, then descent all the way back to Imlil. It's a long, tiring day.

Many walkers add an extra day to acclimatise, either by spending a night in the valley first or by taking the climb more slowly. It is time well spent.

Rules on guiding have changed in recent years, and visitors climbing Toubkal have been required to go with a licensed mountain guide. Check the current rules before you go; your accommodation or a reputable local guide will know.

## Guides and mules

Hiring a local, licensed mountain guide is the single best decision you can make in the Atlas. They know the routes, the weather and the people, they speak Tamazight (the Amazigh language spoken in these mountains) as well as Arabic and usually French and English, and they can adjust plans on the day if conditions change.

Mules and muleteers are central to trekking here. They carry luggage and supplies, and they're a vital source of income for mountain families. As with camels in the desert, look for an organiser who treats the animals well, with sensible loads.

## When to go

- **Spring (roughly April to June)** brings green terraces, blossom and flowing streams. Snow may linger on the higher passes until late spring.
- **Summer** is warm in the valleys but pleasant at altitude, and popular for Toubkal. Afternoon thunderstorms can build over the peaks.
- **Autumn (roughly September to November)** is lovely, with settled weather and the walnut harvest.
- **Winter** brings snow to the high peaks. Toubkal in winter is a serious mountaineering undertaking that requires crampons, an ice axe, experience and a guide. Valley walks remain possible, but it's cold.

## Altitude and safety

Toubkal is high enough for altitude sickness to be a real risk. Symptoms include headache, nausea, dizziness and unusual tiredness. Go up gradually, drink plenty of water and tell your guide if you feel unwell. If symptoms get worse, descend.

Beyond altitude, use common sense: check the weather, start early, carry enough water and don't walk alone on high routes. Make sure your travel insurance covers hiking at the altitude you plan to reach.

## What to pack

- Broken-in walking boots or sturdy trail shoes
- Layers: a fleece or insulating jacket and a waterproof shell, even in summer
- Sun hat, sunglasses and high-factor sun cream; the sun is fierce at altitude
- A refillable water bottle and purification tablets or a filter
- A headtorch for early starts on Toubkal
- A sleeping bag liner, for gîtes and refuges
- Modest clothing for the villages: covered shoulders and knees

## Mountain etiquette

The villages of the High Atlas are working communities, not stage sets.

- Ask before photographing people, and accept a no gracefully.
- Greet people as you pass. "Salam alaykum" works everywhere; your guide can teach you a few words of Tamazight.
- Don't hand out sweets or money to children. If you'd like to give something, ask your guide about local schools or associations.
- Take your rubbish back down with you.
