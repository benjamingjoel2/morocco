Marrakech has a reputation for being overwhelming, and for the first few hours it can be. The noise, the motorbikes in lanes barely wider than your shoulders, the sheer number of people who want to help you find your way. But give the city two unhurried days and it starts to make sense. Here is how we would spend them if a friend were arriving tomorrow.

## Before you land

A little preparation makes the arrival far gentler.

- **Arrange your airport transfer in advance.** Many riads will organise a driver, and the last stretch to the door is usually on foot with someone pushing your bags on a handcart. You will not find a medina riad on your own at night.
- **Get some dirhams.** The dirham is a closed currency, so you generally can't buy it before you travel. There are cash machines at the airport and around the city; card acceptance in the souks is patchy.
- **Download an offline map.** Signal drops in the narrow lanes, and offline maps help, though even they lose the plot in the densest parts of the medina.

## Day one: arrive slowly

### Morning or afternoon: settle into your riad

A riad is a traditional house built around an inner courtyard, often with a fountain or a few orange trees. Most guesthouses in the medina are converted riads, and the moment you step inside, the street noise falls away. Accept the mint tea you will almost certainly be offered. It is the national gesture of welcome, and drinking it gives you twenty minutes to get your bearings.

Ask your hosts to mark a few landmarks on your map: the nearest main street, the route to Jemaa el-Fnaa, and a reliable taxi point. They know the shortcuts and which turns tend to confuse first-timers.

### Late afternoon: the Koutoubia and its gardens

Walk to the Koutoubia Mosque, whose 12th-century minaret is the city's most recognisable landmark and a useful compass point when you are lost. As in most Moroccan mosques, non-Muslims can't go inside, but the gardens around it are pleasant in the late light, and you will hear the call to prayer carry across the rooftops.

### Dusk: Jemaa el-Fnaa

From the Koutoubia, it is a short walk to Jemaa el-Fnaa, the great square at the heart of the medina. UNESCO recognises it as a cultural space of oral heritage, and that is the right way to think about it: it is a stage for storytellers, musicians, healers and performers as much as it is a public square.

The square changes through the day, but dusk is when it comes alive. Food stalls are set up in rows, smoke rises from the grills, and circles of people gather around Gnawa musicians. A few honest pointers:

- If you photograph a performer, expect to be asked for a tip. Have small coins ready, or keep the camera down.
- You may see snake charmers and monkeys on chains. Many travellers choose not to pay for photos with animals, and we'd encourage you to do the same.
- At the food stalls, follow the locals. Stalls that are busy and turning food over quickly are usually the safest bet. Agree on what you're ordering before you sit down.

For a calmer view, climb to one of the café terraces overlooking the square and watch the lights come on over a glass of tea.

## Day two: palaces, gardens and souks

### Early morning: the souks before the crowds

The souks to the north of Jemaa el-Fnaa are a maze of covered lanes, loosely organised by trade: leather, metalwork, lanterns, spices, woven goods. Go early, before about ten, when shutters are still going up and the lanes are quieter. You will see artisans at work and feel far less pressure to buy.

On getting lost: it will happen, and it's part of the experience. If someone offers to guide you somewhere you didn't ask to go, a polite "la, shukran" (no, thank you) and a confident walk in any direction usually works. If you need directions, ask a shopkeeper rather than someone who approaches you.

On bargaining: it's expected in the souks, but it should be good-humoured. Decide what an item is worth to you before you start, don't name a price you wouldn't pay, and walk away graciously if you can't agree.

### Late morning: the Ben Youssef Madrasa

This former Koranic school is one of the most beautiful buildings in the city, with carved cedar, stucco and zellige tilework around a central courtyard and pool. The students' small cells upstairs give you a sense of what life here was like. Check opening times locally, as they can change.

### Lunch: something slow-cooked

Marrakech's signature dish is **tanjia**, beef or lamb slow-cooked for hours in a tall clay urn, traditionally left in the embers of a hammam furnace. Look for it at simple places near the souks. If tanjia isn't on offer, a tagine or a bowl of harira will do nicely. If you'd like someone to show you the food of the medina, see our [things to do](/things-to-do/).

### Afternoon: the Bahia Palace and the Mellah

Head south through the medina to the Bahia Palace, built in the 19th century as a grand residence for a vizier. It's all painted ceilings, courtyards and fountains, and it rewards a slow wander. Nearby is the Mellah, the historic Jewish quarter, with its spice market and quieter, more residential feel.

If you prefer greenery to grandeur, take a taxi out to the **Jardin Majorelle**, created by the painter Jacques Majorelle and later owned by Yves Saint Laurent and Pierre Bergé. Its cobalt-blue villa and cactus gardens are popular, so book tickets ahead and go early or late in the day.

### Evening: a hammam

End your second day the way many Moroccans end their week: in a hammam. You can choose between a simple local hammam, where you bring your own black soap, scrubbing glove and towel, or a spa-style hammam with more privacy and a higher price. Either way, the steam, the scrub and the quiet afterwards are the best possible cure for two days of walking.

## A few practical notes

- **Dress:** Light, loose clothing that covers your shoulders and knees is comfortable and respectful in the medina, for all genders.
- **Taxis:** Petits taxis are the small cars used within the city. Ask for the meter or agree on a fare before you set off.
- **Pace:** The midday heat can be intense for much of the year. Plan indoor visits or a riad siesta between roughly one and four.
- **Water:** Most travellers stick to bottled or filtered water.
